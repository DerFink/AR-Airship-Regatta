[b]RC Airship Regatta[/b]
Open-source simulator for DIY racing airships. Built with the Godot Engine 4.

[b]Equations of motion (6 degrees of freedom)[/b]
The airship is a rigid body with 3 translations and 3 rotations. The simulator works in body axes (origin at the centre of buoyancy, nose forward) at 60 steps per second. Velocity v and rotation rate ω form the state ν = [v; ω]; all masses are collected in a full 6×6 mass matrix M:

[code]M = M_RB + M_A
p = M·ν = [p1; p2]
M·dν/dt = [ F − ω × p1 ;  T − ω × p2 − v × p1 ][/code]

M_RB: mass and inertia of the ship (envelope, lifting gas, motors, battery, fins) about the centre of buoyancy – with the full inertia tensor (products of inertia of the attached parts) and the coupling m·r_G between translation and rotation, because the centre of gravity lies below the centre of buoyancy
M_A: added (virtual) masses of the air moved along (hull and fins)
p1, p2: linear and angular momentum of ship and entrained air
F, T: forces and moments from weight, buoyancy, hull, fins and motors
The equation is solved with a Cholesky factorisation of M, which is only recomputed when the ship is rebuilt. If computing power runs short, the simulator falls back to a simplified calculation using only the diagonal of M.

[b]Added masses[/b]
An airship is almost as light as the air it displaces. When it accelerates it has to push that air along – much more sideways than lengthwise. For the hull the simulator uses Lamb's coefficients for the prolate ellipsoid (ATTAS approach):
[code]axial: k1·ρ·V      lateral: k2·ρ·V      rotation: k'·I_air[/code]
At L/D = 6, k1 ≈ 0.05 and k2 ≈ 0.92: sideways the ship drags almost a second load of air.
Each fin also carries air along perpendicular to its surface, like a flat plate (c chord, b span):
[code]m_F = ρ·π/4·c²·b[/code]
Because the fins sit far aft, this mainly raises the inertia in pitch and yaw and couples sideways motion with rotation.

[b]Munk moment[/b]
The unequal added masses directly produce the M_A part of the term [code]−v × p1[/code] in the moment equation. For the hull at an angle of attack α, approximately
[code]M_Munk = ½·ρ·v²·V·(k2 − k1)·sin 2α[/code]
This moment turns the nose further out of the wind – the bare hull is unstable. Because the boundary layer at the tail cancels part of it, the simulator uses 60 % of the theoretical value.

[b]Fins and rudders[/b]
Each fin is a small wing with its own local flow (airspeed + rotation of the ship). Lift follows Helmbold for low aspect ratios, with stall at large angles; control effect depends on the type (flap, all-moving, flexible Depron plate). Fins behind the centre of gravity stabilise, canards at the front destabilise.

[b]Profiled fins and Reynolds number[/b]
Tail fins and canards can be flat plates (e.g. Depron) or symmetric NACA 00xx sections (last two digits = thickness in % of chord; Chewie: NACA 0020). How a section behaves depends on the Reynolds number, which the simulator computes every step from airspeed and mean fin chord:
[code]Re = v · c / ν      (ν = 0.000015 m²/s)[/code]
Chewie's fin (c ≈ 19 cm): 2 m/s → Re ≈ 25,000, 6 m/s → 76,000, 10 m/s → 127,000 – tiny compared with aircraft (millions).
[b]Flat plate:[/b] the sharp leading edge makes the flow separate early and abruptly (≈ 14°), almost independent of Re. Blunt edges add drag.
[b]NACA 00xx:[/b] the round nose keeps the flow attached longer: stall later and softer, the thicker the more (0009 ≈ 18°, 0020 ≈ 24° at Re 10⁵). At low Re the laminar boundary layer separates earlier – stall comes a little sooner and thick sections lose some lift slope.
[b]Zero-lift drag:[/b] grows with thickness and at low Re (friction ∝ 1/√Re).
The values are approximations from published low-Reynolds measurements, transferred to the small aspect ratios of the fins.

[b]Thrust[/b]
Each motor pushes along its axis; swivelling motors rotate that axis. Thrust drops with airspeed (propeller inflow) and with battery voltage. A motor away from the centre of gravity also adds a moment.

[b]Weight and buoyancy[/b]
[code]Buoyancy = ρ_air · V · g      (ρ = 1.20 kg/m³)[/code]
The remaining mass (battery, ballast) is sized and placed so that the ship is slightly heavier than air and hangs level. With the centre of gravity below the centre of buoyancy, a pendulum moment rights the ship.

[b]Gertler hull[/b]
The envelope shape comes from the DTMB Series 58 (Gertler, 1950), implemented after Johannes Eissing's Gertler Shape Generator. A 6th-order polynomial gives the radius along the length; parameters are length/diameter (L/D), position of maximum diameter, nose and tail radius and prismatic coefficient. Volume, surface, centre of buoyancy and added masses follow from the shape.

[b]Masses[/b]
Envelope (spread over the surface), motors and fins where they are mounted, electronics and battery underneath the hull. From these the simulator computes centre of gravity and moments of inertia; the remaining mass is trimmed automatically.

[b]Tail size[/b]
The builder checks the tail volume [code]V_H = S_fins · lever arm / V_hull[/code]. If it is too small, the tail cannot balance the Munk moment and a warning appears.

[b]Your own models[/b]
Under “Model & Build” → “Custom build” you can set hull, tail, propulsion and appearance freely. Models can be exported as a file and sent to the admin – checked models are added to the selection for everyone.

[b]Race scoring (F7B rules)[/b]
The race is 3 laps of a figure eight around the two pylons with a flying start. The total time t over all 3 laps is measured. To compare large and small airships fairly, the time is weighted by the size of the ship:
[code]L_ref = (L · W · H)^(1/3)
t_scored = t · √(L_ref / 1 m)[/code]
L, W, H: length, width and height of the overall box (hull, fins, motors, battery) in m – as shown in the builder. L_ref is the edge length of a cube with the volume of the box (FAI Sporting Code F7B), so a smaller ship gets a bonus. The display on the right during the flight shows the laps, the total time and the scored time.

[b]The virtual flight controller[/b]
It mirrors the Teensy flight controller (dRehmFlight) of the real Chewie. It measures rates and attitude (like gyro and accelerometer) and drives fins and swivelling motors. Throttle always stays with the pilot – the controller never changes thrust.

[b]PID controller[/b]
[code]u = Kp·e + Ki·∫e dt + Kd·de/dt[/code]
e is the error between setpoint and measurement. P reacts immediately, I removes steady errors (limited against wind-up), D damps fast changes.

[b]Modes[/b]
[b]Direct[/b]: sticks go straight to fins and swivels, no controller.
[b]Angle[/b]: pitch as attitude controller (stick = target angle, release = level), yaw as rate controller.
[b]Horizon[/b]: like Angle, yaw with PID rate controller (max. 110 °/s), pitch protection limits the pitch angle, roll via the swivelling motors.

[b]Mixer[/b]
Each fin gets pitch and yaw shares according to its mounting angle. Swivelling motors take pitch together and roll in opposition.

[b]Demo[/b]
The autopilot flies the figure eight around the pylons with the current model and current controller values – only through the sticks, like a pilot. It can fail, e.g. when the battery runs empty or the ship loses the track.
@@@
[b]Battery model[/b]
Default: LiPo 4S, 300 mAh (Chewie).

[b]Voltage under load[/b]
The open-circuit voltage E depends on the state of charge (typical LiPo curve per cell). With internal resistance R and motor power P:
[code]V = E − I·R,   I = P(V) / V[/code]
hence [code]a·V² + V − E = 0[/code] with [code]a = R·P0 / V0³[/code].

[b]Power[/b]
[code]P_motor ≈ 25 W · (thrust in N)^1.5[/code]   plus 2 W for electronics and servos. These are assumptions and can be replaced by measurements.

[b]Effect on flight[/b]
As voltage drops, the motors spin slower: [code]thrust ∝ (V/V0)²[/code]. An empty battery gives no thrust.

Rough flight times (Chewie straight ahead): 30 % throttle ≈ 12 min, 50 % ≈ 3 min, 70 % ≈ 77 s, full ≈ 34 s. “Reset” recharges the battery.
